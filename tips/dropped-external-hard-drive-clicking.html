Hard drives · After a drop · Clicking, beeping, not spinning

Dropped the drive,
and now it clicks?

Samuel, Owner & Technician · November 22, 2026

Unplug it and stop powering it on. A hard drive that clicks, grinds or beeps after a drop may well be damaged inside, and every extra start-up risks making the files harder to get back.

Seagate’s recovery service is blunt about it: if a drive is making unusual noises, shut it down immediately and do not power it up again. Clicking, buzzing and scraping can mean mechanical failure, and running a drive in that state can do further damage to the drive and its data. So the useful work right now isn’t testing. It’s knowing what kind of drive you have, writing down what happened, and finding out where else the files might already be. A dropped drive that’s quiet but doesn’t show up gets one careful check, covered below. A dropped drive that clicks is also the kind I send to a data-recovery specialist instead of opening here, which is one more reason to leave it switched off.

For an external hard drive, or a laptop with a spinning hard drive inside, that was dropped or knocked over and now clicks, beeps, won’t spin or doesn’t show up. A drive that went missing with no drop and no strange noise is a calmer problem, linked below.

01 / Stop first

Unplug it, and leave it off

Don’t plug it back in to see whether it’s better. Seagate’s recovery service says that if a drive is making unusual noises or showing other obvious signs of distress, you should shut it down immediately and not power it up again. Its drive troubleshooter says the same about grinding: power the drive down right away, to avoid further damage that would make recovery more difficult, if not impossible.

  1. Unplug itThe USB cable, and the power adapter if it has one.
  2. Write it downSeagate says to note every symptom and what happened, before you forget.
  3. Look for copiesCloud accounts, the computer the files came from, an old phone.
  4. Ask before testingA text costs nothing. Another start-up might.

Why the rush to do nothing? Inside a hard drive, the heads that read your files sit a hair above platters that spin rapidly. After a hard knock, running the drive can let a damaged part do more damage. Seagate warns that with a grinding or scratching sound, continued use can physically damage the platters, and the platters are where the data is.

If it’s a laptop with a hard drive inside, the same rule covers the whole laptop. Shut it down. Don’t keep restarting it to see if it boots this time, and don’t repair or reinstall Windows from an install disc to get it booting: Seagate warns that a Windows repair is potentially data destructive and will very probably erase the drive.

02 / Which kind of drive

First, is it a spinning hard drive or an SSD?

Everything above is about drives with moving parts. Seagate’s summary: hard disk drives make sound because of their spinning parts, while solid-state drives have no moving parts and are generally quieter. To tell which you have:

  • It clicked, whirred or vibrated, now or before the drop: it’s a hard drive. An SSD has nothing inside that can click.
  • Read the label. Many say HDD or SSD. If yours doesn’t, search the model number from another device.
  • Size is a hint, not proof. A desktop drive with its own power adapter is typically a hard drive. A small, very light pocket drive is often an SSD.

An SSD has no heads or platters, so a drop affects it differently: the case, the port or the circuit board can be damaged, but nothing inside can grind. A dropped SSD that doesn’t show up gets the one check below, and then the same stop rule.

03 / Normal versus stop

Normal hard drive sounds, and the sounds that mean stop

Hard drives are never silent, which makes it easy to tell yourself it always sounded like that. Seagate sorts the sounds into two groups:

ExpectedA working drive
  • A steady whir, louder while it spins up or winds down
  • An occasional soft click or chatter while reading and writing
  • One hard click as the heads park, at shutdown or sleep
StopUnplug it
  • Hard clicking that persists, or comes with a repeating clunk
  • Grinding, scratching or clanking
  • Beeping
  • Any sound that changed after the drop

Seagate describes hard clicking as a very noticeable sound, like metal hitting metal, and says it usually indicates a physical failure. Then it adds a condition: if nothing traumatic happened to the drive first, treat the click as the soft kind and troubleshoot it. A drop is the traumatic thing. After one, assume the worse reading.

Beeping has a milder explanation on a drive that was never dropped. Seagate says external drives only beep when something is wrong, and that in most cases a single intermittent beep means the drive isn’t getting enough power. After a drop, treat it as a stop sound anyway: a beep with no spin can also come from inside the drive, and finding out which means more start-ups. Mention the beep when you text.

A drive that makes none of these sounds, whether it spins up normally or stays silent, is the one case with a check worth doing.

04 / One check, once

The one check that’s safe, and when to skip it

Skip this section if the drive clicks, grinds, scrapes or beeps.

It’s only for a dropped drive that makes no abnormal sound and doesn’t show up.

  1. Change one thing: the cable. Use a different cable you know works, plugged straight into the computer, not through a hub. Seagate and WD both list the cable and an underpowered port as reasons a drive won’t spin up, and WD adds that long or third-party cables may not carry enough power for a portable drive.
  2. Connect it once and listen. A smooth spin-up and a drive that appears is the good result.
  3. If it appears, copy the files that matter most, right away, to a different drive. Don’t scan or repair it first. If it stalls, disconnects or starts clicking, stop.
  4. If it clicks, beeps or never appears, unplug it. That was the test. One more port or one more computer is one more start-up.

Seagate’s own troubleshooter draws the same line: once a different port, cable and computer haven’t helped and you need the data, it’s a data-recovery question.

A drive that was never dropped, makes no odd noise and is simply missing on a Mac has more steps you can safely try: external drive not showing up on a Mac.

05 / Don’t make it worse

What not to do with a dropped hard drive

  • Don’t format or initialize it when the computer offers. A damaged drive can show up as unreadable, and the computer’s suggestion is to erase it. Seagate says not to format the drive or change the partitions, because either action can result in permanent loss of your data. Its USB troubleshooter repeats the warning in capitals: formatting erases all data on the drive.
  • Don’t run recovery or repair software on a noisy drive. Seagate says that if a drive makes any unusual noises, do not attempt to use any type of utility software, and that do-it-yourself software makes use of the problem drive, potentially causing complete failure. First Aid and CHKDSK count.
  • Don’t open it. Seagate says hard drives are particularly sensitive to contamination and should only be opened in a microscopically clean environment.
  • Don’t freeze it, tap it or shake it. None of those repairs anything, and a second jolt is still a jolt. WD’s handling rule is short: do not drop or jolt the drive.
  • Don’t keep trying. Another computer, another day, one last time. Seagate’s wording is that hasty attempts to fix the problem can often result in further damage and data loss.
06 / Who does what

What I can do here, and what I send out

Data recovery isn’t one job, and you should know which part happens on my bench.

What I can often do here
  • Save the data from a drive that’s beginning to fail but still partly works
  • Save the data from a drive with a damaged port or another circuit-board problem
What I send to a specialist
  • Drives whose internal mechanism is damaged
  • Drives whose file system is badly damaged

A dropped drive that clicks, or that still won’t spin after the one check, is the internal-mechanism kind. It will most likely go to a data-recovery specialist, not get opened in Hailey. That’s exactly why not powering it on matters: a specialist can only work with what’s still intact, and running a damaged mechanism puts that at risk.

A dropped drive that’s quiet and still partly readable, or one where only the port or the circuit board took the hit, is often one I can handle. The first step is the same text either way, and I’ll tell you which kind it sounds like.

07 / The honest answer

Is the data gone?

Not necessarily, and nobody can tell from the sound alone. Seagate’s recovery service says there are occasions when the damage is permanent and a complete recovery isn’t possible. Which way yours goes can’t be known until the drive has been examined, and anyone who promises a result before that is guessing.

Two things are in your hands:

  • Don’t add damage. That’s everything above.
  • Find the copies you forgot about. Photos may already be in iCloud Photos, Google Photos or OneDrive. Files may still be on the computer or phone they came from, in sent email, or in an older Time Machine or File History backup. If the important ones turn up, the dropped drive stops being an emergency.

Once the files are safe, back up before a repair covers how to keep a second copy from now on.

08 / Send the story first

What to note for a recovery quote

  • The drive: brand, model and capacity from the label, and whether it’s a hard drive or an SSD.
  • What happened: how far it fell, onto what, and whether it was plugged in and running at the time.
  • What it does now: clicking, grinding, beeping, silent, or spinning but not showing up.
  • What you’ve tried since, and roughly how many times it’s been powered on.
  • What’s on it, and which folders matter most.
  • Whether any backup or other copy exists, even an old one.
  • Whether the drive is encrypted or password-protected.

If you bring it in, keep the drive in its enclosure, with its own cable and power adapter, padded so it can’t take a second knock. That’s enough for me to tell you what’s likely and what happens next. Text it to 208-450-1606, or see data recovery.
